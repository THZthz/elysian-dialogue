## Film Is a Cross-Section of Real Life

A story — regardless of format — is not told from a character's birth to their death. It is a cross-section cut from a complete life: at a specific moment, because of a specific event, this person is forced into an inescapable situation.

This means:

**1. Before the story begins, the world has already been running for a long time.**
Characters have a past (Ghost). The world has an era and context. Relationships have accumulated history. The first page of the story is not the character's first day — the character walks into the first scene carrying all their past. This past does not need to be fully shown to the audience, but the storyteller must know it.

**2. The story starts from the middle.**
The audience is thrown into a world that is "already happening." A good opening is not "starting from the beginning" but "cutting in at the moment of maximum tension." The audience reconstructs the causes through the character's actions and reactions — this is more compelling than chronological exposition.

**3. The opening IS the attitude.**
The first scene defines the entire work's temperament — not the theme, but "how does this story see the world?" A commercial opening declares rhythm through high-density plot action. A literary opening declares style through a passage of precise life texture. Regardless of genre, the opening must be one of the most carefully designed sections of the entire work.

**4. The storyteller's job is to build the complete world first, then make the cut.**
The logic of the creative process is therefore: first build (past, world, character in full), then cut (choose where to start telling, what to show and what to hide). It's not "what do I want to tell" — it's "this world exists, and I choose which window to let the audience look through."
