## Action-Reaction: The Basic Unit of Shot Design

### Core Logic
The most basic unit of shot (group) design is: **one action (cause) shot(group) + one reaction (result) shot(group)**.

"Action" refers to anything that triggers change — a line of dialogue, a behavior, the appearance of information. "Reaction" refers to the impact this event has on the surrounding world — another character's expression, environmental change, silence itself.

The action-reaction pairing is the fundamental way audiences understand narrative causality. But it is not a rigid "ABAB" pattern — this basic unit has more than ten combinatorial variants, and the choice depends on narrative purpose.

### Variant Patterns

**Standard: Action → Reaction**
The most basic form. A does something → B reacts. A line spoken → the other person's expression. A door opens → the person inside.
Suitable for: conventional narrative where information is clear and causality is direct.

**Reaction-First: Reaction → Action**
First show the reaction (shocked expression, shattered cup), then reveal the action that triggered it (message on screen, person standing at the door).
Effect: Creates suspense — audience sees the "result" first, then the "cause." The few seconds between are the strongest curiosity window.

**Action Juxtaposition: Action A → Action B → Action C → Reaction**
Multiple actions stacked before a single cumulative reaction. Example: a news story broadcast simultaneously across multiple countries (multiple action shots) → final shot of someone turning off the TV (reaction).
Effect: Accumulated pressure — the reaction's weight is amplified by the preceding action stack.

**Reaction Omission: Action → (no reaction) → Next scene**
Deliberately NOT showing the reaction — character says something, then instead of cutting to the listener's reaction, cut directly to the next scene.
Effect: liubai (intentional blank space / artistic omission). The audience fills in the unshown reaction in their mind, and the imagined reaction is more powerful than any image. Can also create suspense: "the consequences of this line won't manifest until later."

**Action Hidden (Sound-Action): (Sound Action) → Visual Reaction**
The action does not appear visually, only as sound — a gunshot (offscreen) → character's expression change. A phone call's audio → character's silence after hanging up.
Effect: Sound-image separation, letting the audience's imagination fill the visual gap. Sound-actions are often more terrifying and impactful than visual ones.

**Reaction Delayed: Action → Normal → Normal → Reaction**
After the action occurs, the character's reaction is not immediate — a stretch of seemingly normal daily behavior intervenes (cooking, walking, working), then the reaction erupts in an unguarded moment.
Effect: Realism. Human emotional reactions are often not instant — when the impact is too great, people need time to "digest."

**Synchronous: Action and Reaction in Same Frame**
No cut — a single long take where action and reaction happen simultaneously in the frame. Foreground character does something while background character reacts at the same time.
Effect: The audience chooses who to watch. Also increases frame information density and realism.

**Reaction Chain: Action → Reaction A → Reaction B → Reaction C**
One action triggers a chain of reactions from different characters/elements. Example: news announced → first person stands up → second person smashes a glass → third person remains silent.
Effect: Shows theradiating range of an event, and simultaneously shows different characters' different attitudes toward the same event.

**Repeated Action Gradation: Action → Reaction → Same Action (slight variation) → Different Reaction**
The same action repeated, but fine-tuning each time, and the reaction shifts accordingly. Example: character rings a doorbell three times — first press light / waiting, second press heavy / anxious, third press desperate.
Effect: Shows time passage and emotional accumulation through repetition with variation.

**Contrapuntal: Action A (Visual) + Action B (Audio) → Composite Reaction**
The frame shows one action while the sound conveys a different action — two different pieces of information act on the audience simultaneously through sound-image counterpoint. Example: visual is character smiling and applauding while audio is their internal monologue saying the exact opposite.
Effect: Reveals the contradiction between surface and interior.

**Omitted Action: Reaction → Reaction → Reaction (Action Never Shown)**
The action itself is never shown from beginning to end — the audience only sees multiple characters' reactions to something, reconstructing what happened through those reactions.
Effect: Makes "that thing" more terrifying/significant than any image could convey. Classic horror technique — don't show the monster, only show the faces of people who have seen it.

**Chain-Linked: Action A → Reaction A (which IS Action B) → Reaction B (which IS Action C) → ...**
One shot simultaneously is the reaction of the previous group AND the action of the next group — Character B sees what A did (reaction), then B's reaction itself triggers a new event (action), C reacts to B's behavior, C's reaction triggers D... Shot groups never break; narrative advances like dominos.
Effect: Creates unstoppable narrative momentum — once the event starts, it cannot be stopped. This is the most natural way to connect shot groups, merging independent groups into a fluid narrative section. Also the most common structure in ensemble scenes — a message transmitted through a crowd, each person's reaction simultaneously being the next person's stimulus.

### 70% Conventional vs 30% Standout

Based on empirical data analysis, the usage of action-reaction patterns follows the 70/30 rule — 70% of shots use conventional patterns to build the narrative skeleton; 30% use standout patterns to create memory points.

#### 70% — Conventional Patterns (Building the Narrative Skeleton)

**Standard (Action→Reaction) ~30%**: The most basic causal unit, workhorse of daily narrative. Character does something → world responds. Nothing flashy, but clear, reliable, audience can follow.
When to use: Anywhere clear causality needs to be established. A line → the other's expression. A decision → the decision's consequence.

**Action Juxtaposition (A+B+C→Reaction) ~15%**:workhorse of montage sections. Multiple similar actions stacked → one summary reaction.
When to use: Need to show scale, need to show capability, need to show timespan.

**Progressive Group (Action→Intensification→Intensification) ~15%**: Same type of action or phenomenon continuously escalating. Each shot more extreme than the last.
When to use: Need to create acceleration and absurd accumulation.

**Chain-Linked (Reaction A which IS Action B) ~10%**: One shot simultaneously is reaction of previous group AND action of next group. Narrative never breaks, like dominos.
When to use: Sections within a scene that need sustained forward momentum. Especially suitable for ensemble scenes and causal chains on the timeline.

#### 30% — Standout Patterns (Creating Memory Points)

**Reaction Omission ~5%**: Deliberately not showing reaction; hard cut to next scene or black screen.liubai (intentional blank space / artistic omission) lets audience fill in their own imagination.
When to use: The heaviest moments. Relationshipshattering → pure black 4 seconds. Person disappears → empty chair. Work ends → "Good luck" without showing fate. Omitted reactions are more powerful than any image. **Use 1-2 times per work. More is laziness.**

**Action Hidden (Sound-Action) ~5%**: Frame does not show the action; action exists only in sound. Frame stays on reaction or irrelevant image.
When to use: Sections needing sound-image separation to create tension.

**Reaction-First ~5%**: Show result/conclusion first, then trace back to cause. Audience first sees "what happened to this person," then spends the entire work understanding "why."
When to use: Opening (open with conclusion, then flashback), reversal sections.

**Contrast Juxtaposition ~5%**: Two actions forming visual contrast — split screen, cross-cutting, or object comparison within same frame. No dialogue needed to explain the contrast relationship; let the audience see it themselves.
When to use: Sections needing visual commentary.

**Visual Spectacle Ultimate Reaction ~3%**: All accumulated actions throughout the work converge at the end into one overwhelming visual image. This image is the entire work's "answer."
When to use: Climax or ending. **Use 1-2 times per work only.** The power of this shot comes from the accumulation of all preceding setup — spectacle without setup is just effects.

**Opening-Closing Loop ~2%**: The ending reaction precisely echoes the opening action — same line, same composition, same action, but because everything in between has happened, the meaning is completely different.
When to use: The very end of the work. **Use only once.**
