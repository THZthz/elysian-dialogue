## Cut Connection Logic

Shots are not randomly sequenced — every cut has visual logic. The previous shot's visual content, shot scale, and angle determine the next shot's "entry point."

### Shot Scale Connection Principles

**Adjacent-Scale Smooth Transition**: Most cuts occur between adjacent shot scales (wide → mid, mid → close-up); the audience does not feel visual jarring. Data: approximately 60% of cuts are within one scale jump.

**Cross-Level Jump Cut Impact**: Jumping directly from wide to close-up (or vice versa) produces strong visual impact — this is not an error but a weapon. Use at moments requiring surprise, reversal, or emotional fracture. Cross-level jumps of 2+ scales account for approximately 15% of cuts, all appearing at key narrative nodes.

**Same-Scale Continuity Stability**: Continuous use of the same shot scale (mid → mid → mid) creates a feeling of stability, calm, or suppression — depending on content. Most common in dialogue scenes. But 4+ consecutive same-scale shots risk monotony and need intentionality.

### Movement Direction Within a Scene

**Push-In (Far → Near)**: Within a scene, gradually push from wide to mid to close-up, creating a feeling of "progressively deepening" — from environment into character, from external into internal. Push-in accounts for 54% of within-scene scale changes, the most natural narrative direction.

**Pull-Out (Near → Far)**: Pull from close-up to wide, creating a feeling of "detachment" or "revealing a larger space." Often used at scene endings — after the character finishes speaking, the camera pulls back, leaving the audience in the space to digest. Also used at moments of suddenly revealing environmental information.

**Bounce (Push-in → Pull-out → Push-in)**: Example: "Mid → Close-up → Mid" or "Wide → Close-up → Wide" — first push in to emphasize a detail, then bounce back to show reaction or environment. This is the most common three-shot sequence pattern.

### Scene Transition Cut Logic

**Wide Establishing Method**: Open a new scene with a wide or extreme wide shot, letting the audience know "we have arrived at a new place." The most reliable approach.

**Detail Opening Method**: Open a new scene with a close-up — first see a detail (a hand, an object), then reveal the space through subsequent shots. Creates suspense and curiosity.

**Emotional Bridge Method**: The last shot of the previous scene and the first shot of the next scene have emotional or visual echo/resonance — using visual texture to bridge emotion.

**Hard-Cut Contrast Method**: Deliberately create strong contrast between the visual styles of two scenes. The contrast itself IS narrative.

**Match Cut Method**: A visual element from the previous shot matches a visual element in the next shot. Smooth but don't overuse.

### Shot Group Internal Connection Logic

**Montage Group**: Maintain the same or similar shot scale within the group; advance through content variation. Primarily hard cuts, even rhythm.

**Progressive Group**: Shot scale progressively pushes in — wide → mid → close-up, information layering.

**Causal Group**: Action → reaction alternation. As events heat up, shot scale can progressively push in.

**Contrast Group**: Hard cuts create contrast — shot scale can be same or cross-level.

### Angle Variation Principles
Approximately 60% of cuts maintain the same angle. Angle changes appear at moments of power/psychological relationship shifts, entering new scenes, or when needing to break visual fatigue. **Simplicity priority** — angle changes are seasoning, not the main dish.
