## Literary Film Inner Beat Table

Suitable for: character-driven films, auteur cinema, stories not dependent on strong plot. The beats are not external events but the character's internal state transition points. External events may be very small (a glance, an offhand remark, a walk), but they trigger an earthquake in the character's interior.

### Generic Inner Beat Structure (6 steps)

**1. Normal + Crack**
The character's daily life. But a certain detail hints at unease beneath the surface. The setup doesn't rush to advance — it lets the audience dwell in the character's world, establishing atmosphere through sensory details (sound, light, objects).

**2. Disturbance**
A small event breaks the character's self-deception. The literary film's catalyst is typically not a dramatic major event but a subtle moment that makes the character "unable to continue pretending everything is normal."

**3. Drifting & Avoidance**
The character tries to return to the original life, but the crack has already appeared. Many "gap moments" — the character alone, spacing out, doing irrelevant things. These gaps are not wasted scenes — they show what the character is avoiding.

**4. Confrontation**
The character is forced to face what they've been avoiding. It could be a quiet conversation, a reunion after long separation, or simply standing alone somewhere and discovering they are crying.

**5. Choice or Acceptance**
The literary film's climax is usually not "what was done" but "what was accepted." The character either accepts a painful truth or decides to continue living in the lie. Both endings are valid — the key is the weight of this choice.

**6. Aftershock**
The ending is not an answer but a new question. The literary film's Final Image is typically open — the character has changed, but the consequences of that change have not yet arrived.

### Style Variant 1: Kore-eda (Daily Flow)

- Setup is extremely slow. No clear "inciting incident" — the crack is not opened by a single event but slowly seeps out through accumulated daily details.
- Confrontation is not direct conflict but emotions suddenly leaking in a plain, mundane moment — a sentence stopped halfway, suddenly walking faster, turning to look at a position that's already empty.
- The ending remains on an extremely quotidian action — putting away dishes, turning off the light, walking home. The world hasn't changed, but the audience knows something already has.
- Dialogue is extremely quotidian. Characters never discuss "big things," but the shadow of big things is always present.
- Rhythm is like breathing — even, slow, without sudden change.

### Style Variant 2: Wong Kar-wai (Emotional Flow)

- Narrative does not pursue causality — connections between scenes are emotional rather than logical. After one scene ends, the next scene doesn't need to "continue" the previous scene, only needs emotional echo/resonance or contrast.
- Extensive repetition of visual motifs — the same space filmed repeatedly at different times, the same action repeated by different characters. Repetition creates the texture of time.
- Characters speak their interior through voiceover, but a gap exists between voiceover content and visual content — talking about one thing while the image shows another. The gap between the two IS subtext.
- No traditional climax. The story reaches a saturation point of emotion at a certain moment, then quietly concludes.
- Time is the protagonist — jumping timelines, expired dates, phones that no longer ring.

### Style Variant 3: Ozu (Still-Life Flow)

- Extremely low camera position, fixed shots, almost no camera movement. The frame itself is static; only the people within it move.
- "Pillow shots" — empty frames inserted between scenes: corridors, rooftops, distant chimneys. These empty frames don't narrate; they create pauses where the audience's emotions settle.
- Family relationships are the eternal motif. Conflict does not come from external events but from the unbridgeable understanding gap between generations — parents must ultimately accept their children's departure; children can never fully comprehend their parents' sacrifice.
- Characters are extremely polite and restrained on the surface, but restraint itself is the greatest subtext.
- The ending is typically a kind of "acceptance" — not joy, not sorrow, but a quiet "indeed, it is so."

### Style Variant 4: Linklater (Dialogue Flow)

- Almost entirely driven by dialogue. Dialogue is not a tool; dialogue IS the story.
- The rhythm of dialogue simulates real conversation — it digresses, circles back, suddenly becomes serious then suddenly funny.
- Characters naturally expose their interior through chatting — not through "confession" but through what they choose to talk about, what they avoid, which topic they linger on for an extra second.
- Temporal and spatial constraints instead create intimacy — two people with limited time must decide what is worth saying.
- The climax is a single line that lands exactly right — after all the setup, when a certain sentence is spoken the audience knows "everything is different now."
- The ending is typically open — the conversation has ended, but the relationship still hangs in the air.

### Style Variant 5: Bong Joon-ho (Genre Hybrid)

- The skeleton is strict commercial structure (can use Save the Cat or Story Circle as foundation), but social critique and black humor are stuffed beneath the genre's skin.
- The first half and second half can be completely different genres — the mutation of genre IS part of the narrative.
- Space IS metaphor — the character's physical position (up/down, inside/outside, above ground/below ground) directly corresponds to social position.
- Details carry multiple meanings — the same object or action is a comedy prop in the first half and becomes a horror/tragedy prop in the second half.
- The ending is an emotional bomb covered by satire — on the surface a cold joke, underneath despair.
- If the direction is "the shell is genre but the core is social issues," recommend this variant.
