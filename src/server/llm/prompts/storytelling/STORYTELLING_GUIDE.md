## Core Principle: A Cross-Section of Real Life

A story—whether a short film or a feature—is not about a character's life from birth to death. It is a cross-section cut from a complete life: at a specific moment, because of a specific event, this person is forced into an inescapable situation.

This means:

**1. Before the story begins, the world has already been running for a long time.**
The character has a backstory (Ghost), the world has its historical context, and relationships have prior accumulations. The start of the story is not the character's first day—the character walks into the first scene carrying all their backstory. This backstory does not all need to be shown to the player, but the GM must know it.

**2. The story starts in the middle.**
The player is thrown into a world that is "already happening." A good opening is not "starting from the beginning," but "cutting in from the most tense cross-section." The player deduces the cause through the character's actions and reactions, which is more engaging than chronological exposition.

**3. First build the complete world, then make a cut.**
First construct (backstory, worldbuilding, full character profile), then crop (choose where to start telling the story, what to show and what to hide). It's not "what do I want to tell," but "what exists in this world, and which window do I choose for the player to look through."

## Dramatic Action: The Smallest Unit of Story

**Definition:** Dramatic Action = Goal + Conflict.
It's not "a person wants to drink water," but "a dehydrated person searches for water in the desert." The goal must have an urgent pursuit, and the obstacle must be in direct opposition to the goal.

Every scene in the story should contain at least one micro dramatic action (Goal → Obstacle → Outcome). The outcome can be success, failure, or an unexpected turn. The micro dramatic actions within scenes converge upward to form the core dramatic action at the paragraph level, and the core dramatic actions at the paragraph level converge upward to form the core dramatic action of the entire piece.

**Testing Criteria:**
- Is the goal reasonable and urgent?
- Is there a strong conflict relationship between the goal and the obstacle?
- Is this conflict sufficient to drive the story forward?

## Character Design: Rejecting Cardboard Characters

### Want vs Need

- **External Want**: The goal that drives action, something the character is consciously aware of and actively pursues
- **Internal Need**: The gap in the character's soul they are not yet aware of, usually the deep-seated reason for the external want

The tension between Want and Need is the engine of the character arc. At the beginning of the story, the character pursues their Want; by the end, they either attain their Need (growth arc) or reject their Need (tragic arc/anti-arc).

### Character Arc

Arc = an irreversible change from State A to State B.
In short content, the arc is a single leap (A→B).
In long content, the arc may go through multiple "false changes"—the character appears to have changed but reverts, until the final true transformation.

### Character Biography

A character's backstory is not background introduction but the root of conflict. Key events in the biography should explain:
- Why the character has this Want
- Why the character cannot see their own Need
- Where the character's contradictions come from

### Contradiction

A good character has at least one layer of contradiction: outward appearance vs. inner truth.
For example: outward confidence vs. inner inferiority, outward indifference vs. inner desire for connection.
Contradiction makes a character three-dimensional and provides space for subtext.

## Audiovisual Writing

### Show, Don't Tell

It is strictly forbidden to write psychological descriptions ("he felt," "she realized"). Only write actions that can be seen and sounds that can be heard. If an emotion cannot be conveyed to the player through the character's physical behavior, then that emotion should not appear in the script.

**Wrong Example:** "He finally understood his father's painstaking efforts."
**Correct Approach:** He stood there watching for a moment, then took off his jacket and draped it over his father.

### Subtext

Dialogue is an "iceberg"—the words the character speaks are only the surface; the true meaning lies beneath the water. Avoid on-the-nose dialogue. Use actions, pauses, evasions, and topic shifts to express the real meaning.

**On-the-nose Dialogue (Wrong):**
> "I'm so afraid of losing you."

**Dialogue with Subtext (Correct):**
> "Take that jacket with you when you leave. ...It's cold outside."

### Action as Subtext

All emotional conveyance must be capturable by the camera. Replace any explanatory dialogue with the character's specific actions. A good story, even if all the dialogue is removed, should still allow the audience to roughly understand what is happening.

### Dialogue Style

- Colloquial, like real people talking, not writing an essay
- Different characters have different ways of speaking (word choice, sentence length, habitual expressions)
- Avoid excessive metaphors, analogies, and literary-sounding AI-like phrasing
- Convey information naturally within conversations; do not let characters say things they both already know

## Self-Check System

### Self-Check 1: Core Concept & Dramatic Action
- [ ] Has a "Goal + Conflict" been established?
- [ ] Is the goal reasonable and urgent?
- [ ] Is there a strong relationship between goal and obstacle?
- [ ] Is the conflict the engine driving the story?
- [ ] Is the obstacle at a reasonable scale, rather than artificially exaggerated?

### Self-Check 2: Character Building & Arc
- [ ] Are the external want and internal need clear and in opposition?
- [ ] Has an irreversible internal change been presupposed?
- [ ] Does the ending demonstrate a "new normal"?
- [ ] Does the character possess contradictions?
- [ ] Does the behavior align with the logic of the character biography?

### Self-Check 3: Structure & Pacing
- [ ] Are the structural sections clear?
- [ ] Is the inciting incident well-defined?
- [ ] Is the climax clear?
- [ ] Does every part advance the plot or reveal character?
- [ ] Are there any ineffective plot points?

### Self-Check 4: Scene Writing & Audiovisual Language
- [ ] Is there any psychological description? (Fail if yes)
- [ ] Are there any parenthetical hints? (Fail if yes)
- [ ] Is the story told through visuals?
- [ ] Is the dialogue too on-the-nose?
- [ ] Are subtext and body language utilized?
- [ ] Is the dialogue colloquial?
- [ ] Are characters "explaining the setting" rather than "reacting to the setting"?

## Dual-Track Rhythm System

The rhythm of a good story is a balance of tension and release. Rhythm is not a single line—it has two parallel running tracks, which can be synchronized or offset. Offset itself is a powerful narrative tool.

### External Plot Rhythm

External plot rhythm is the "density and intensity of events"—how many things happen in a scene, how intense the conflict is, how much information is conveyed.

- **Tight**: High-density events, rapid cutting, multi-person dialogue, chases, confrontations, revelations
- **Loose**: Everyday scenes, solitary moments, transitions, landscapes, silence

The ebb and flow of external rhythm requires contrast—continuous tension numbs the player, continuous relaxation causes the player's mind to wander. Rule of thumb: after every high-intensity plot segment, a period of "breathing" is needed for the player to digest.

### Internal Emotional Rhythm

Internal emotional rhythm is the "fluctuation of the character's emotional state"—the magnitude of emotional rise and fall the character experiences, and how much emotion the player is asked to invest.

- **Heavy**: Confessions, betrayal, loss, fear, ecstasy, despair
- **Light**: Banter, everyday chit-chat, small humor, quiet companionship

Emotional rhythm and plot rhythm are not always synchronized. Some of the most powerful scenes are precisely those where the two are offset:

- **Loose Plot + Heavy Emotion**: Two people sit quietly eating a meal, nothing happens, but the player knows one of them is leaving tomorrow. The calmer the picture, the heavier the emotion.
- **Tight Plot + Light Emotion**: An intense car chase scene, but the soundtrack is a light pop song, and the characters are quipping. Tense events are wrapped in light-hearted moods.
- **Tight Plot + Heavy Emotion**: Climax sequence—dense events and emotional explosions. This is the peak of the entire piece and cannot last too long.
- **Loose Plot + Light Emotion**: Pure breathing room scenes. Let the player rest. Usually placed before or after the climax.

### Rhythm Wave Diagram

Check whether the external plot intensity and internal emotional intensity in the scenes have sufficient fluctuation and contrast.

The two lines do not need to be synchronized, but should avoid:
- Both lines remaining at a low level for a long time (player has nothing to watch and nothing to feel)
- Both lines remaining at a high level for a long time (player feels fatigued, the climax becomes unexceptional)
- Either line rising monotonously without ever falling back (no sense of breathing room)
