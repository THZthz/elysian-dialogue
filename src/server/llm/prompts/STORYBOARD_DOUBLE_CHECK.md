## Storyboard Double Check (Three Layers)

After completing shot breakdown for each scene, go back and do a three-layer check. All scene types must pass this check.

### Layer 1: Director Tone Consistency
- Does this scene's shot language match the director's tone-setting?
- Is the image system present where it needs to be?

### Layer 2: Scene Rhythm Matching
- Do cutting frequency, shot scale choices, and duration allocation match the pacing plan?
- Is there meaningful contrast or transition with adjacent scenes' rhythm?

### Layer 3: Standard vs Standout Ratio

Most shots are handled with clean conventional approaches — this is fine. But if a scene is 100% "standard treatment," it has no memory points.

**Standard Shots (~70-85%)**: The narrative skeleton. They are reliable, clear, and don't draw attention to the "filmmaking" itself:
- Character presentation (largest volume): Use conventional shot scales and angles to show what characters are doing, saying, expressing. No need for fancy composition.
- Information establishment: Use wide or mid shots to establish space, time, character relationships.
- Plot advancement: Use causal group standard editing to drive events forward.
- Emotional rendering: Use standard approaches matching the director's tone-setting.

Standard shots' key principle: **clean, reliable, don't steal the spotlight.** Their function is to keep the audience's attention on the story, not the filmmaking.

**Standout Shots (~15-30%)**: Memory points. If you could only show the audience one frame, they would remember the entire scene because of this image.

**Six Main Standout Techniques (by usage frequency):**

**1. Visual Metaphor (~38% of standout shots)**: Use a concrete image to convey abstract meaning. Not "character feels trapped" but "empty wall surface with only light and shadow."
Action-reaction pattern: Usually appears as a "non-standard reaction" to a standard action.

**2. Perspective Reversal / Reveal (~22%)**: Suddenly change the audience's understanding of known information.
Action-reaction pattern: Usually uses reaction-first or omitted-action patterns.

**3. Structural Echo / Closed Loop (~12%)**: Form precise visual or narrative echo/resonance with another shot elsewhere in the work.
Action-reaction pattern: Itself is a "response" — responding not to the previous shot but to some shot from the work's opening.

**4. Sensory Contrast / Juxtaposition (~9%)**: Place two strongly contrasting elements in the same frame or adjacent shots.
Action-reaction pattern: Two "actions" juxtaposed; the audience completes the "reaction" themselves (making the contrast judgment in their mind).

**5. Information Delay / Liubai (intentional blank space / artistic omission) (~9%)**: Deliberately not giving the audience the information they want; using suspense to create tension.
Action-reaction pattern: Typical use of the reaction-omission pattern.

**6. Visual Spectacle / Sublimation (~3-4%)**: Pure visual impact, usually appearing near the ending.
Action-reaction pattern: Typically the entire work's final "reaction" — the ultimate visual response to the entire story.

### Standout Shot Usage Patterns (from empirical data)

**Position distribution**: Standout shots are not concentrated in any one section — they are dispersed throughout the work, with slightly more at the opening (establishing the work's temperament) and ending (sublimation / closed loop / narrative circularity). Mid-section standout shots typically appear at turning points.

**Neighbor characteristics**: 82% of standout shots are surrounded by standard shots before and after — standout shots are embedded within standard narrative, relying on the surrounding "normal" to contrast by opposition their own "abnormal." The probability of two consecutive standout shots is only 18%.

**Duration characteristics**: Standout shots average slightly longer than standard shots (4.9s vs 4.1s) — standout images need more dwell time for the audience to "see" their unique quality.

**Ratio by work type**:
- Structurally strong / auteur works: standout ratio can reach 30%
- Narrative-driven works: standout ratio ~6-10% is sufficient
- Concept / absurdist works: standout ratio ~15-23%

### Selection Check
- Does each scene have at least one standout design? (Even if just an unusual shot scale choice or one instance of spatial interjection)
- Is the standout design placed on the shot with the heaviest narrative purpose?
- Does the standout design have a narrative reason? ("It looks cool" is not a reason. "Use overhead shot to make the character look trapped" is a reason.)
- Are there more than two consecutive standout shots? (More than two and they stop being "standout" — they become the new normal.)
