## Foreshadowing & Payoff

### What Is Foreshadowing
Casually placing a detail (dialogue, prop, action, image) in the early part of the story, then having that detail reappear with greater significance later.

### Four Levels of Foreshadowing

- **Prop Foreshadowing**: An object appearing as mundane in the early section becomes a key prop later. Example: a lighter bought casually, used in Act 3 to ignite an escape signal.
- **Dialogue Foreshadowing**: A line that was small talk in the early section gains entirely new meaning later. Example: "What I fear most is silence" — character description in Act 1, becomes echo/resonance when the character faces silence alone in Act 3.
- **Behavioral Foreshadowing**: A habitual action hints at the character's Ghost. Example: a character always leaves the door cracked open — later revealed they were once locked in a room.
- **Structural Foreshadowing**: A seemingly irrelevant scene later proves to be key information. Example: a passerby's conversation in the opening, proven to be the real hidden driver/behind-the-scenes manipulator in the ending.

### Foreshadowing Rules
- On first appearance, foreshadowing must not feel deliberate — the audience should not realize "this is foreshadowing"
- Every planted foreshadowing must have a payoff — foreshadowing without payoff is broken contract
- On payoff, the audience should feel "so that's what that was" — the payoff should change how they understand the earlier information
- A feature should have at least 3-5 foreshadowing/payoff pairs distributed across different levels
